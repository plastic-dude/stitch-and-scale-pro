## Governing product goal

The repository constitution (`docs/goals-and-agent-goals.md`) defines the north star as a trustworthy bridge from design measurements to professional, multi-size, publication-ready work while preserving mathematical integrity, designer ownership, recoverability, and creative control. The emotional test is: “I made it. I understand it. I can trust it. I can show it.” The archive validation report sharpens this into: help an independent designer turn a base-size pattern into a checked, human-review-ready, publishable multi-size pattern with less rework and lower production cost.

The product is not primarily a general knitting dashboard, a grading calculator, a PDF button, a marketplace, a social network, or a generic AI assistant. The defensible wedge is a production-control layer between creative drafting and professional publication. The recommended core path is `Import/Draft → Grade → Check → Handoff → Publish/Export`; business modules remain secondary until the core earns trust.

## Evidence-backed product requirements

The archive repeatedly prioritizes an auditable calculation trail: inputs, gauge, ease, formula, rounding, constraints, standard/provider version, manual overrides, affected sizes, and engine version. It recommends explicit states for `Calculated`, `Automated check passed`, and `Human reviewed`, rather than a single credibility score that could be mistaken for certification.

It recommends a professional technical handoff packet containing grading assumptions, full measurement table, schematics, issue list, unresolved warnings, test-knit notes, version number, and designer sign-off. A proper human-review workflow should support annotations, issue assignment, comments, version comparison, approval, and a final approval record without silently mutating the original.

The local-first promise is a trust advantage but browser-clearing risk remains significant. A 10/10 state requires visible backup status, deterministic versioning, restore testing, automatic or otherwise reliable recovery, and exact privacy language. Cloud sync and OAuth should follow proof of the core workflow unless cross-device identity is itself being tested.

The archive also calls for a guided import-first path because many target designers already use spreadsheets, a deterministic downloadable PDF rather than a browser print handoff, conservative economics covering all production inputs, and a clear known-issues/changelog surface.

## Beta and launch strategy

The supplied beta plans recommend a private, invitation-based first cohort of roughly 10–15 qualified testers, focused on active independent pattern publishers and a smaller number of technical editors/experienced graders. The single beta question is whether a designer can start with a base-size design, create a credible multi-size graded pattern, identify problems, and export a usable draft without losing confidence.

The recommended first-beta architecture is local-first project data plus a small server-side control plane: email reservation, invitation, lightweight verified identity, server-side feature entitlements, feedback collection, abuse protection, and audit events. Do not unlock premium features with a local browser flag. Do not build full cloud sync or multiple OAuth providers before the core workflow proves value.

Feedback should be a quiet persistent in-app button and short modal with type, expected/actual result, route, app version, limited browser context, optional redacted diagnostics, optional screenshot, contact permission, and a report ID. Reports should feed a secure database record, immediate notification for P0/P1 issues, and a private role-protected admin inbox. The archive explicitly advises against a large public comment wall, full ticketing platform, automated AI classification, or elaborate analytics during the first beta.

Private-beta expansion should require evidence such as at least 70% unassisted core-workflow completion, zero unresolved P0 issues, a median trust score of at least 8/10, repeat-use intent around 60% or better, meaningful real-pattern attempts, and qualified willingness to pay. These are decision targets, not industry benchmarks.

## Pricing and customer caution

The archive warns that “average-income knitting mothers” and professional independent pattern designers are not automatically the same customer. A hobbyist path should be simple, project-priced, and low-risk; active creators may justify recurring pricing through advanced QA, version history, handoffs, testing, and business tools. Price figures in the archive are hypotheses requiring paid-pilot validation, not conclusions.

The strongest launch promise recommended by the archive is: “A calmer, more transparent way to turn a knitwear design into a reviewable multi-size pattern draft.” It warns against leading with “AI-powered all-in-one knitwear business platform,” because breadth weakens credibility and attracts the wrong users.
