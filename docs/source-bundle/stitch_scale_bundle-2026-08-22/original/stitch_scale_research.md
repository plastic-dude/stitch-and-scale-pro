## Workflow source: Sister Mountain, How to Structure Your Knitting Pattern Design Process
Source: https://www.sistermountain.com/blog/knitting-pattern-design-process

The author describes pattern design as a long process that can span several months and uses a standard process to reduce overwhelm, estimate time, meet seasonal deadlines, and improve release consistency. Early steps include mood board (~2 hours), ordering yarn (30 minutes to 4 hours, sometimes seeking yarn support), knitting a stockinette swatch (~2 hours), sketching (1–4 hours, sometimes 50+ sketches), and designing/knitting a stitch. The source emphasizes that structured timelines and checklists help designers see dependencies and work on multiple patterns while tech editing or test knitting is underway.

Implication for Stitch & Scale: the product should not start at the grading table only; it can create value by structuring the pattern production pipeline, but the highest defensible wedge remains the later technical stage where grading, testing, and publishing create costly errors.

## Marketplace economics and setup evidence

Etsy official Fees & Payments Policy: https://www.etsy.com/legal/fees/ . Etsy charges a $0.20 USD listing fee per item; a 6.5% transaction fee on the displayed price plus shipping/gift wrap; payment-processing fees vary by bank-account location; offsite-ad-attributed orders may incur 12% or 15%; Etsy Plus is $10/month; Etsy Pattern is $15/month after its trial. This makes low-priced digital patterns sensitive to fixed fees, payment fees, and advertising.

Payhip official pricing: https://payhip.com/pricing . Free plan is $0/month plus 5% transaction fee; Plus is $29/month plus 2%; Pro is $99/month with no Payhip transaction fee. PayPal/Stripe processing fees still apply. Payhip also states immediate deposits after completed transactions and unlimited products/revenue on all plans.

Ravelry official help search: https://www.ravelry.com/help/search?query=selling+patterns . Ravelry’s setup requires joining the designer/shopkeeper workflow, configuring a pattern store, connecting PayPal, and using PayPal as the payment processor. Direct guide links may require login, so exact current percentage fees were not independently confirmed from the protected page.

Implications: A pattern designer may sell a $5–$18 PDF, but the economic bottleneck is not only creation time; it includes tech editing, test knitting, sample yarn, photography, layout, marketplace/payment fees, launch marketing, customer support, and revisions. Stitch & Scale’s fee and income calculators are useful, but they need conservative assumptions and should visibly include non-platform costs.

## Income distribution source

Media Peruana / Knitting by Kristen Jancuk: https://www.mediaperuana.com/blog1/designerincome . The article analyzes January 2019 Ravelry data for 10,059 independent designers. It reports 7,277 designers (72.3%) sold no more than $50 that month; using January as an average would imply nearly three-quarters earned no more than $600/year before Ravelry and PayPal fees. It reports that $201+ in January was roughly the top 10%, $1,001+ was about 3%, and $2,001+ about 1.6%. The author reports $7,910.30 gross income in 2019 across several revenue streams and $4,647.13 profit after sample yarn, tech editing, advertising, software, PayPal, Ravelry, and other expenses.

Caveat: this is older, self-reported/secondary analysis and Ravelry-only monthly data, not a current representative industry survey. It is still useful for showing that most independent designers have low and uneven income, while a small minority earn meaningful revenue.

Implication: Stitch & Scale should not promise income growth merely from better grading. Its defensible economic promise is reducing avoidable production cost and increasing release capacity/quality, with revenue improvement treated as an outcome to measure rather than a guaranteed claim.

## Setup and production costs source

Slow Knitting, How do you become a knitting pattern designer?: https://www.slow-knitting.com/news/2024/9/27/how-to-become-a-knit-or-crochet-pattern-designer . Reported ranges include yarn support of $0–$200; technical editing of $30–$300 depending on complexity, with simple one-size accessories around $30–$65; layout design of $0–$200; photography of $0–$500+; test knitting often free in exchange for a pattern but requiring management; and sample knitting at a cited rate of $0.25 per yard. The article gives a concrete example of $28 yarn plus $50 testing/tech editing, or about $78 production cost, and notes more expensive shoots and book projects.

Implication: the most painful losses are often hidden labor and rework, not only platform fees. A pattern that sells for $8–$18 can be uneconomic if it requires $100–$300 in editing, $100+ in sample labor, and many hours of designer time. Stitch & Scale should calculate break-even using all production inputs, not just hourly design time and marketplace fees.

## Competitor and alternative-tool evidence

EnvisioKnit official site: https://www.envisioknit.com/ . It positions itself as knitting pattern design software accessible to beginners and professionals, with chart editing, motif manipulation, written-pattern preview, stitch/row repeat detection, and export to SVG, PDF, Word, OpenOffice, PNG, JPEG, or bitmap. It offers a downloadable demo and a one-time purchase model, and supports Mac and Windows.

Stitchmastery official site: https://stitchmastery.com/ . It is positioned around designing knitting charts and instructions, with free download/trial and paid activation, plus chart and written-instruction workflows.

Competitive implication: Stitch & Scale should not compete as a generic chart editor. Its clearest whitespace is an independent designer’s multi-size product-production system: measurement-based grading, visible calculations, structured checks, break-even economics, publishable pattern packages, and versioned evidence of what was checked. The key differentiation must be narrower and deeper than “many features for knitwear designers.”

## Spreadsheet workflow evidence

Sister Mountain, How to Grade Knitting Patterns Using a Spreadsheet: https://www.sistermountain.com/blog/grade-knitting-patterns-spreadsheet . The workflow uses columns for each size, rows for body measurements, ease, garment measurements, gauge, stitch and row counts, shaping, and pattern-specific calculations. Inputs include size labels, body measurements, ease, gauge, and design-specific measurements. Manual work includes unit conversion, copying formulas, choosing size ranges, adjusting features such as necklines/shoulder drops/angles, refining for fit/style, and maintaining reusable templates. The article explicitly says grading can stop designers from publishing and that doing the math across many sizes feels overwhelming. It also suggests dropdowns, conditional formatting, standardized percentage grading, integrated shaping, and reusable automation.

Implication: this is unusually strong evidence that Stitch & Scale is solving a concrete workflow pain. The product should preserve spreadsheet import/export and make every automated formula inspectable, because many users will have existing spreadsheet systems and distrust black-box grading.

## Technical-editing and residual-error evidence

Sister Mountain, A Beginner’s Guide to Working with Tech Editors: https://www.sistermountain.com/blog/tech-editing-beginners-guide . A tech editor checks correctness, concision, usability, math, clarity, sizing, and style-guide consistency, but does not rewrite the pattern; the designer makes the corrections. The recommended workflow is to send the first draft and schematic after the sample is completed, often before test knitting, so testers work from a cleaner draft. Badly written patterns generate substantial pattern-support time; test knitters cannot substitute for tech editors because they may not read every word or catch all inconsistencies. The source stresses that designers should not self-edit exclusively because they are too close to the work.

Implication: a 10/10 solution must be positioned as a tech-editor preparation and evidence system, not as a replacement for human tech editing or test knitting. It should produce an issue list, severity, affected size/section, exact calculation trail, change log, and a final handoff packet for a human reviewer.

The Council describes a broad U.S. audience of more than 50 million people who know how to knit, crochet, and craft, and reports high repeat participation among surveyed crafters. This is evidence that the underlying craft ecosystem is large, but it does not imply that all participants are potential Stitch & Scale customers.

The broader Ravelry community is reported at over 9 million accounts. These figures imply a large maker audience and a much smaller professional/semiprofessional designer segment, which supports a focused B2B-ish tool rather than a mass consumer app.

Implication: segment by behavior, not identity. The best initial customer is an active pattern publisher with at least one planned release and enough pattern complexity that spreadsheet grading, tech editing, testing, and PDF production are painful.

## Current yarn-consumer context

Craft Industry Alliance, Yarn Consumer Survey Results 2025: https://craftindustryalliance.org/the-size-of-the-yarn-market-yarn-consumer-survey-results-2025/ . The report says 6,300 consumers responded; average age was 58.8, average household income $100,000, 51% retired, and 28% employed full-time. It reports average online spend around $81 for ages 34–44 and about $70 for ages 65–74; ages 55–64 had the highest average in-person spend at just over $66. Respondents started 19 projects per year and completed just over 16, an 84% completion rate.

Caveat: this is a consumer survey, not a designer-income survey, and its average household income is not representative of every maker. It does show a large, active purchasing ecosystem but should not be used to assume that a designer customer has high disposable income.

## Break-even scenario calculations

Using Payhip’s 5% free-plan transaction fee as a simple platform-fee illustration, and excluding payment processing, a $78 simple-pattern production cost requires approximately 11 sales at an $8 price, 7 sales at $12, or 5 sales at $18. A $250 typical-sweater production cost requires approximately 33 sales at $8, 22 at $12, or 15 at $18. A $500 complex multi-size pattern requires approximately 66 sales at $8, 44 at $12, or 30 at $18. These figures exclude the designer’s unpaid labor and therefore understate economic break-even.

Time-value reference calculations: 20 hours at $15/hour equals $300; at $25/hour equals $500; at $35/hour equals $700. Forty hours equals $600, $1,000, or $1,400 at those hourly rates, respectively. Eighty hours equals $1,200, $2,000, or $2,800.

Interpretation: most low-priced patterns do not become profitable quickly unless the designer self-performs labor, receives yarn support, gets free testing, sells enough units, or builds a catalogue whose backlist continues to sell. A product that cuts preventable rework or reduces paid technical-editing time can create value even when it does not increase unit sales.
